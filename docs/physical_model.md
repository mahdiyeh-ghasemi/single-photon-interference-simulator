# Physical Model: Single-Photon Double-Slit Interference

## 1. Objective

This document establishes the physical and mathematical framework for a computational simulation of single-photon interference in a double-slit experiment.

The model describes the relationship between quantum probability amplitudes, spatial probability distributions, individual photon detection events, and the statistical emergence of an interference pattern.

The simulation has three primary objectives:

1. Calculate the theoretical double-slit interference distribution.
2. Simulate individual photon detection events and their sequential accumulation.
3. Analyze the statistical properties of photon counts.

The baseline model assumes coherent illumination and does not include which-path measurements.

## 2. Physical Configuration

Consider a monochromatic photon with wavelength $\lambda$ incident on two identical, parallel slits.

The physical parameters are:

- $a$: width of each slit.
- $d$: center-to-center distance between the two slits.
- $L$: distance between the slits and the detection screen.
- $\lambda$: wavelength of the incident photon.
- $x$: transverse coordinate on the detection screen.

The centers of the two slits are located at

$$
x_1=-\frac{d}{2},
\qquad
x_2=+\frac{d}{2}.
$$

The simulation uses the Fraunhofer diffraction approximation:

$$
L\gg\frac{a^2}{\lambda},
\qquad
L\gg\frac{d^2}{\lambda}.
$$

For small diffraction angles, the transverse wave-vector component is approximated by

$$
k_x\simeq\frac{2\pi x}{\lambda L}.
$$

## 3. Single-Slit Diffraction Amplitude

For a uniformly illuminated slit of width $a$, the far-field diffraction amplitude is proportional to the sinc function.

The single-slit diffraction amplitude is given by

$A_{\mathrm{slit}}(x)=\mathrm{sinc}\left(\frac{\pi ax}{\lambda L}\right)$

The normalized sinc function is defined as

$$
\mathrm{sinc}(u)=\frac{\sin(u)}{u}
\quad \text{for } u\neq 0
$$

and

$$
\mathrm{sinc}(0)=1.
$$

This function describes the diffraction envelope generated by the finite width of each slit.

## 4. Double-Slit Probability Amplitude

The total probability amplitude is obtained by coherently adding the contributions from the two slits.

The phase difference between the two paths is

$$
\Delta\phi(x)=\frac{2\pi dx}{\lambda L}.
$$

The total amplitude can therefore be expressed as

$$
\psi(x)\propto
A_{\mathrm{slit}}(x)
\left[
e^{-i\pi dx/(\lambda L)}
+
e^{i\pi dx/(\lambda L)}
\right].
$$

Using the identity

$$
e^{-i\theta}+e^{i\theta}=2\cos\theta,
$$

the resulting amplitude becomes

$$
\psi(x)\propto
2A_{\mathrm{slit}}(x)
\cos\left(\frac{\pi dx}{\lambda L}\right).
$$

This expression represents the coherent superposition of the probability amplitudes associated with the two possible paths.

## 5. Quantum Probability Density

According to the Born rule, the probability density of detecting a photon at position $x$ is proportional to the squared modulus of the probability amplitude.

$$
P(x)\propto|\psi(x)|^2.
$$

Consequently, the theoretical interference intensity is

$$
I(x)=I_0
\mathrm{sinc}^2\left(\frac{\pi ax}{\lambda L}\right)
\cos^2\left(\frac{\pi dx}{\lambda L}\right).
$$

Here, $I_0$ is an overall intensity scale.

The two factors have distinct physical meanings:

- The sinc-squared term describes the single-slit diffraction envelope.
- The cosine-squared term describes the interference fringes.

For a finite detection-screen interval from $-X_{\max}$ to $+X_{\max}$, the normalized probability density is

$$
p(x)=
\frac{I(x)}
{\displaystyle\int_{-X_{\max}}^{X_{\max}}I(u)\,du}.
$$

The normalized probability density satisfies

$$
\int_{-X_{\max}}^{X_{\max}}p(x)\,dx=1.
$$

This distribution provides the probability model used to generate individual photon detection positions.

## 6. Individual Photon Detection

In the simulation, each photon is detected at one localized position on the screen.

The position of the $n$-th photon is sampled according to

$$
x_n\sim p(x).
$$

For a sequence of $N$ independent photon detections, the recorded positions are

$$
\{x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_N\}.
$$

Each detection corresponds to a single localized event.

No individual photon is represented as a fraction of a detection event distributed across the entire screen.

The interference pattern emerges from the statistical accumulation of these individual events.

## 7. Sequential Accumulation of Photon Detections

The accumulated detection events after $N$ photons can be represented by the empirical detection measure:

$$
H_N(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\delta(x-x_n).
$$

Here, $\delta(x-x_n)$ represents a localized detection event at position $x_n$.

For numerical implementation, the detection screen is divided into discrete spatial bins, and the number of photon detections in each bin is recorded.

The normalized empirical detection distribution is obtained by dividing the number of detections in each bin by the total number of detected photons and the bin width.

As the number of independent detection events increases, the normalized histogram approaches the theoretical probability density:

$$
\hat{p}_N(x)\longrightarrow p(x)
$$

This convergence is understood statistically and is associated with the law of large numbers.

The simulation demonstrates this behavior by displaying cumulative detection histograms at different photon counts.

The progressive formation of the interference pattern is therefore a consequence of repeated quantum measurements.

## 8. Photon Counting Statistics

### 8.1. Spatial Bin Probabilities

The detection screen is divided into $M$ spatial bins.

The probability that a photon is detected in bin $i$ is

$$
p_i=\int_{x_i}^{x_{i+1}}p(x)\,dx.
$$

For bins covering the entire modeled screen interval, the probabilities satisfy

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{M}p_i=1.
$$

### 8.2. Multinomial Counting Distribution

For a fixed total number of $N$ independently detected photons, the probability of obtaining counts $n_1,n_2,\ldots,n_M$ in the corresponding bins follows a multinomial distribution:

$$
P(n_1,\ldots,n_M)=
\frac{N!}{n_1!n_2!\cdots n_M!}
\prod_{i=1}^{M}p_i^{n_i}.
$$

The total number of detected photons is constrained by

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{M}n_i=N.
$$

The expected photon count in bin $i$ is

$$
\mathbb{E}[n_i]=Np_i.
$$

The corresponding variance is

$$
\mathrm{Var}(n_i)=Np_i(1-p_i).
$$

### 8.3. Poisson Counting Distribution

If photon arrivals are modeled as an independent Poisson process, the number of detections in bin $i$ follows a Poisson distribution:

$$
P(n_i)=
\frac{\mu_i^{n_i}e^{-\mu_i}}{n_i!}.
$$

Here, $\mu_i$ denotes the expected number of detections in bin $i$.

For a Poisson distribution, the mean and variance are equal:

$$
\mathbb{E}[n_i]=\mu_i.
$$

$$
\mathrm{Var}(n_i)=\mu_i.
$$

The multinomial and Poisson models correspond to different assumptions about photon counting and arrival statistics.

## 9. Numerical Validation

The numerical implementation should satisfy the following physical and statistical validation criteria.

### 9.1. Probability Normalization

The numerically calculated probability density must satisfy

$$
\int_{-X_{\max}}^{X_{\max}}p(x)\,dx\simeq 1.
$$

### 9.2. Spatial Symmetry

For identical slits under symmetric illumination, the theoretical distribution must be symmetric around the central axis:

$$
p(-x)=p(x).
$$

### 9.3. Interference Fringe Spacing

The approximate distance between adjacent interference maxima is

$$
\Delta x=\frac{\lambda L}{d}.
$$

### 9.4. Diffraction Envelope

The simulated interference pattern must exhibit the expected sinc-squared diffraction envelope.

### 9.5. Statistical Convergence

As the number of detected photons increases, the normalized detection histogram should approach the theoretical probability density.

### 9.6. Counting Statistics

The simulated photon counts should be consistent with the selected multinomial or Poisson counting model.

## 10. Model Assumptions and Limitations

The baseline model uses the following assumptions:

- Monochromatic photon illumination.
- Two identical and parallel slits.
- Uniform illumination across each slit.
- Coherent superposition of the two slit amplitudes.
- Fraunhofer diffraction approximation.
- Independent photon detection events.
- Ideal detection without detector noise or finite spatial resolution.

The present model does not explicitly include:

- Photon polarization.
- Detector inefficiency.
- Environmental decoherence.
- Which-path measurements.
- Time-dependent Schrödinger equation simulations.

In particular, sequential accumulation refers to the progressive recording of individual photon detection events. It does not represent the time evolution of the quantum wavefunction.

## 11. Conclusion

This physical model establishes the mathematical foundation for a computational study of single-photon double-slit interference.

It connects the coherent superposition of probability amplitudes and the Born rule with individual photon detection events and statistical counting distributions.

The central objective is to demonstrate how an interference pattern emerges through the accumulation of discrete photon detections, consistent with the probabilistic predictions of quantum mechanics.
