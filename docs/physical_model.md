# Physical Model: Single-Photon Double-Slit Interference

## 1. Objective

This document establishes the physical and mathematical framework for a computational simulation of single-photon interference in a double-slit experiment.

The model describes the relationship between quantum probability amplitudes, spatial probability distributions, individual photon detection events, and the statistical emergence of an interference pattern.

The simulation has three primary objectives:

1. Calculate the theoretical double-slit interference distribution.
2. Simulate individual photon detection events and their sequential accumulation.
3. Analyze the statistical properties of photon counts.

The baseline model assumes coherent illumination and does not include which-path measurements.

## 2. Physical Configuration

Consider a monochromatic photon with wavelength $\lambda$ incident on two identical, parallel slits.

The physical parameters are:

- $a$: width of each slit.
- $d$: center-to-center distance between the two slits.
- $L$: distance between the slits and the detection screen.
- $\lambda$: wavelength of the incident photon.
- $x$: transverse coordinate on the detection screen.

The centers of the two slits are located at

$$
x_1=-\frac{d}{2},
\qquad
x_2=+\frac{d}{2}.
$$

The simulation uses the Fraunhofer diffraction approximation:

$$
L\gg\frac{a^2}{\lambda},
\qquad
L\gg\frac{d^2}{\lambda}.
$$

For small diffraction angles, the transverse wave-vector component is approximated by

$$
k_x\simeq\frac{2\pi x}{\lambda L}.
$$

## 3. Single-Slit Diffraction Amplitude

For a uniformly illuminated slit of width $a$, the far-field diffraction amplitude is proportional to the sinc function.

The single-slit diffraction amplitude is given by

$$
A_{\mathrm{slit}}(x)=
\mathrm{sinc}\left(\frac{\pi ax}{\lambda L}\right)
$$

The normalized sinc function is defined as

$$
\mathrm{sinc}(u)=\frac{\sin(u)}{u}
\quad \text{for } u\neq 0
$$

and

$$
\mathrm{sinc}(0)=1.
$$

This function describes the diffraction envelope generated by the finite width of each slit.

## 4. Double-Slit Probability Amplitude

The total probability amplitude is obtained by coherently adding the contributions from the two slits.

The phase difference between the two paths is

$$
\Delta\phi(x)=\frac{2\pi dx}{\lambda L}.
$$

The total amplitude can therefore be expressed as

$$
\psi(x)\propto
A_{\mathrm{slit}}(x)
\left[
e^{-i\pi dx/(\lambda L)}
+
e^{i\pi dx/(\lambda L)}
\right].
$$

Using the identity

$$
e^{-i\theta}+e^{i\theta}=2\cos\theta,
$$

the resulting amplitude becomes

$$
\psi(x)\propto
2A_{\mathrm{slit}}(x)
\cos\left(\frac{\pi dx}{\lambda L}\right).
$$

This expression represents the coherent superposition of the probability amplitudes associated with the two possible paths.

## 5. Quantum Probability Density

According to the Born rule, the probability density of detecting a photon at position $x$ is proportional to the squared modulus of the probability amplitude.

$$
P(x)\propto|\psi(x)|^2.
$$

Consequently, the theoretical interference intensity is

$$
I(x)=I_0
\mathrm{sinc}^2\left(\frac{\pi ax}{\lambda L}\right)
\cos^2\left(\frac{\pi dx}{\lambda L}\right).
$$

Here, $I_0$ is an overall intensity scale.

The two factors have distinct physical meanings:

- The sinc-squared term describes the single-slit diffraction envelope.
- The cosine-squared term describes the interference fringes.

For a finite detection-screen interval from $-X_{\max}$ to $+X_{\max}$, the normalized probability density is

$$
p(x)=
\frac{I(x)}
{\displaystyle\int_{-X_{\max}}^{X_{\max}}I(u)\,du}.
$$

The normalized probability density satisfies

$$
\int_{-X_{\max}}^{X_{\max}}p(x)\,dx=1.
$$

This distribution provides the probability model used to generate individual photon detection positions.

## 6. Individual Photon Detection

In the simulation, each photon is detected at one localized position on the screen.

The position of the $n$-th photon is sampled according to

$$
x_n\sim p(x).
$$

For a sequence of $N$ independent photon detections, the recorded positions are

$$
\{x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_N\}.
$$

Each detection corresponds to a single localized event.

No individual photon is represented as a fraction of a detection event distributed across the entire screen.

The interference pattern emerges from the statistical accumulation of these individual events.

## 7. Sequential Accumulation of Photon Detections

The accumulated detection events after $N$ photons can be represented by the empirical detection measure:

$$
H_N(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\delta(x-x_n).
$$

Here, $\delta(x-x_n)$ represents a localized detection event at position $x_n$.

For numerical implementation, the detection screen is divided into discrete spatial bins. If $n_i$ photons are detected in a bin of width $\Delta x_i$, the normalized histogram density in that bin is

$$
\hat{p}_{N,i}=\frac{n_i}{N\Delta x_i}.
$$

Here, $N$ is the total number of detected photons.

As the number of independent detection events increases, the normalized histogram provides an increasingly accurate estimate of the probability density averaged over each bin. With sufficiently narrow bins and a sufficiently large number of detections, it approximates the theoretical probability density $p(x)$.

This statistical behavior is associated with the law of large numbers.

## 8. Photon Counting Statistics

Photon detection events can be analyzed statistically by dividing the detection screen into discrete spatial bins.

### 8.1 Spatial Bin Probabilities

For a normalized probability density $p(x)$, the probability of detecting a photon within the $i$-th spatial bin is

$$
p_i=\int_{x_i}^{x_{i+1}}p(x)\,dx.
$$

For a screen divided into $M$ non-overlapping bins covering the detection interval, the probabilities satisfy

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{M}p_i=1.
$$

### 8.2 Multinomial Counting Model

When a fixed total number of $N$ independent photons is detected, the bin counts follow a multinomial distribution.

The probability of observing counts $n_1,n_2,\ldots,n_M$ is

$$
P(n_1,\ldots,n_M)=
\frac{N!}{n_1!n_2!\cdots n_M!}
\prod_{i=1}^{M}p_i^{n_i},
$$

subject to

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{M}n_i=N.
$$

The expected count and variance in bin $i$ are

$$
\mathbb{E}[n_i]=Np_i,
$$

$$
\mathrm{Var}(n_i)=Np_i(1-p_i).
$$

Because the total number of detected photons is fixed, the counts in different bins are statistically dependent.

### 8.3 Poisson Counting Model

When photon detections are modeled as independent counting events with a fluctuating total number, the count in each spatial bin can be described by a Poisson distribution:

$$
P(n_i)=\frac{\mu_i^{n_i}e^{-\mu_i}}{n_i!},
$$

where $\mu_i$ is the expected number of detections in bin $i$.

For a Poisson distribution, the mean and variance are both equal to $\mu_i$:

$$
\mathbb{E}[n_i]=\mu_i,
$$

$$
\mathrm{Var}(n_i)=\mu_i.
$$

In this model, counts in separate bins are statistically independent.

The multinomial and Poisson models therefore describe two different counting conditions: a fixed total number of detected photons and a fluctuating total number of detection events, respectively.

## 9. Numerical Validation

The numerical implementation can be evaluated using the following physical and statistical consistency checks.

### 9.1 Probability Normalization

The calculated probability density should satisfy the normalization condition over the detection interval:

$$
\int_{-X_{\max}}^{+X_{\max}}p(x)\,dx=1.
$$

### 9.2 Symmetry of the Interference Pattern

For two identical slits under symmetric illumination, the theoretical probability distribution should be symmetric about the central axis:

$$
p(-x)=p(x).
$$

### 9.3 Fringe Spacing

In the Fraunhofer regime, the approximate separation between adjacent interference maxima is

$$
\Delta x=\frac{\lambda L}{d},
$$

where $\lambda$ is the photon wavelength, $L$ is the distance to the detection screen, and $d$ is the slit separation.

### 9.4 Diffraction Envelope

The interference fringes should be modulated by the single-slit diffraction envelope:

$$
I(x)\propto
\mathrm{sinc}^{2}\left(\frac{\pi ax}{\lambda L}\right).
$$

### 9.5 Statistical Convergence

As the number of independent photon detection events increases, the normalized detection histogram should provide an increasingly accurate approximation to the theoretical probability density.

### 9.6 Photon Counting Statistics

For a fixed total number of detected photons, the simulated bin counts should be consistent with multinomial statistics.

For independent Poisson counting, the sample mean and sample variance of the counts in each bin should become increasingly close to the theoretical Poisson mean $\mu_i$ as the number of repeated trials increases.

These checks provide criteria for assessing the physical consistency and statistical behavior of the numerical simulations.

## 10. Model Assumptions and Limitations

The simulation is based on the following assumptions:

- **Monochromatic illumination:** All photons have the same wavelength $\lambda$.
- **Identical slits:** The two slits have equal widths and are illuminated uniformly.
- **Coherent illumination:** The probability amplitudes associated with the two slits are combined coherently.
- **Fraunhofer diffraction:** The detector is sufficiently far from the slits for the far-field approximation to be valid.
- **Independent detection events:** Individual photon detections are statistically independent and follow the same spatial probability distribution.
- **Ideal detection:** Every incident photon is assumed to be detected, with no detector inefficiency or position uncertainty.

The current model does not include:

- Photon polarization effects.
- Partial coherence or environmental decoherence.
- Which-path measurements or other measurement-induced changes to the interference pattern.
- Detector inefficiency, dark counts, or finite spatial resolution.
- Polychromatic illumination or a distribution of photon wavelengths.
- Time-dependent evolution of the photon wavefunction.

The sequential detection simulation represents the accumulation of individual photon detection events. It does not numerically solve a time-dependent Schrödinger equation or model the physical time evolution of the wavefunction.

These assumptions define an idealized framework for studying single-photon interference and the statistical formation of an interference pattern.

## 11. Conclusion

This physical model describes single-photon interference in a double-slit experiment by connecting coherent probability amplitudes, spatial probability distributions, individual detection events, and photon counting statistics.

The theoretical framework establishes the expected interference distribution under the Fraunhofer approximation. Individual photon detections are then modeled as independent random events sampled from the normalized probability density.

By accumulating these detection events, the simulation demonstrates how an interference pattern emerges statistically, even when photons are detected individually.

The multinomial and Poisson counting models provide complementary descriptions of photon detection statistics under fixed-total and fluctuating-total counting conditions.

Together, these components provide a computational framework for exploring the relationship between quantum probability, individual measurement outcomes, and the statistical emergence of interference.
